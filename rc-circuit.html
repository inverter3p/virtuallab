Understanding RC Circuits: From Analog to Switching

Step 1: The Basic Analog RC Circuit

Let's start with the fundamentals. An analog RC circuit consists of a resistor (R) and a capacitor (C) connected to a constant voltage source (\(V_S\)). When the switch closes, the capacitor begins to charge through the resistor.

The time constant (\(\tau\)) determines how quickly the capacitor charges. It is calculated as:

$$\tau = R \times C$$

The voltage across the capacitor, \(V_C(t)\), at any given time (\(t\)) during charging is described by the following equation:

$$V_C(t) = V_S \left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right)$$

After one time constant (\(t = \tau\)), the capacitor charges to approximately 63.2% of the source voltage.

Step 2: Introducing a Switching Input

Now, what happens if we replace the simple switch and DC source with a continuous square wave? This is a switching circuit. The input voltage rapidly switches between a high state (e.g., 5V) and a low state (e.g., 0V).

Instead of fully charging, the capacitor now repeatedly charges when the input is high and discharges when it's low. This creates a "ripple" in the output voltage across the capacitor.

The average output voltage (the "Target DC Level") depends on the duty cycle of the input signal—the percentage of time the signal is in its high state.

Step 4: Frequency Response Analysis (Bode Plot)

This RC circuit is a low-pass filter. It allows low-frequency signals to pass to the output but attenuates (reduces the amplitude of) high-frequency signals. The Bode plot shows us exactly how much attenuation occurs at each frequency.

What Does This Mean in a Physical Circuit?

Think of the capacitor as a small bucket being filled and emptied. The time constant \(\tau\) determines how fast it fills.

  • Low Frequency: The input switches slowly. This is like leaving the tap on for a long time, then off for a long time. The bucket has plenty of time to fill up and empty, so the output voltage swings wildly. This results in a large voltage ripple.
  • High Frequency: The input switches very quickly. The tap is flicked on and off rapidly. The bucket doesn't have time to fill or empty much before the input changes again. The water level stays relatively stable. This results in a small voltage ripple and a smoother DC output.

The Bode plot's magnitude curve shows this: at high frequencies (right side), the magnitude is low, meaning the output ripple is heavily suppressed.

The Corner Frequency (\(f_c\)) and Switching Circuits

The corner frequency (\(f_c\)) is the critical point where the filter really starts to work. For a good DC power supply made from a switching signal, the goal is to get a smooth output with minimal ripple. Therefore, you want your switching frequency (\(f_{\text{switch}}\)) to be much higher than the circuit's corner frequency.

$$f_{\text{switch}} \gg f_c$$

This ensures the switching signal itself is heavily attenuated, leaving behind the desired average DC level. Go back to the simulator and see how the ripple shrinks as you increase the input frequency far above the corner frequency!
